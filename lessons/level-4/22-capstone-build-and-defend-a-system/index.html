RUNNING CLAUDE AS A SYSTEM · LEVEL 4 · LESSON 27

Capstone: build and defend a system

Build a small AI workflow and defend it: prove with saved evidence that it works for someone other than you, and show it fails safely under a hostile input and a broken step.

Running Claude as a systemLevel 4ProjectProject · several hours

🎯Learning objective

By the end of this project you can build a small AI workflow, prove with saved evidence that it works for someone other than you, and show it fails safely under a hostile input and a broken step.

What this project is

💡Do the build lab first

If you have not already, run the guided build lab before this capstone. It comes in two parts: part 1 walks you through planning and threat-modeling one small workflow on safe synthetic data, scoping it to least privilege, and building the human gate; part 2 adds the privacy-aware log, the kill switch, and the recovery drill. Together they have you practice all six controls once, with no score attached. Do part 1 then part 2 before coming here. The capstone below is where those same controls get defended under attack, so coming in having already done them by hand is the difference between recalling the move and performing it under review.

⚠Warning

There are two paths through this capstone, and only one is a real pass.

Full pass (needs two other people). It needs an independent reviewer who is not you to score it, and a teammate who runs it cold from your written instructions alone. Both are mandatory pass/fail gates: fail either and the capstone fails. Line up both people before you build. This is the only path that earns a real pass.

Pure self-study solo. If you genuinely have nobody to ask, the downloadable scorecard includes a solo substitute. Be clear about what it is: a deliberately weaker fallback that satisfies neither human gate: not the cold teammate run and not the independent review. Running your own workflow is not a teammate cold-starting from your instructions, and a one-day-later self-review is not an independent reviewer, so a solo result is self-assessment / practice-level only, not a real pass. Use it to learn the moves, but do not tell yourself it equals a reviewer who is not you catching the assumptions you cannot see in your own work.

This is a project, not a lesson. There is no new idea to learn here: the capstone exists to make you use, on one real workflow, everything the course already taught: picking the model, writing the prompt, verifying with an eval, planning and gating the risky steps, and wrapping the whole thing in the safety defenses from Lesson 24. You build something small and real, and then you defend it.

“Defend it” is the part that makes this a capstone rather than a demo. Anyone can show a workflow succeeding on the inputs they chose. The bar here is higher and it is deliberately the bar real systems are held to: it has to work when you are not the one running it, and it has to fail safely when someone attacks it or a step breaks. A thing that only works in your hands, on your examples, is not a system, it is a performance.

What the project is actually testing

Four things, and it helps to name them before you build so you build toward them instead of discovering them in review:

  • Does it work at all? Real runs, with the inputs, outputs, and logs saved. Not “I remember it working”: saved evidence.
  • Does it work for someone else? A teammate runs it from your written instructions alone, with you not in the room. This catches every assumption that lived only in your head.
  • Does it fail safely under attack? You feed it a hostile input (a hidden instruction, the untrusted-input attack from Lesson 24) and show it does not obey. Safe failure, not lucky success.
  • Does it fail safely when something breaks? You break a step on purpose (a tool times out, a file goes missing) and show the workflow stops cleanly, the log records it, and you recover from backup.

Notice what is not on that list: a polished result, a clever prompt, a long feature list. The capstone does not reward ambition. It rewards a small workflow you can prove and defend.

How to approach it

The move that saves people is building toward the evidence from the start, instead of building a workflow and bolting evidence on at the end. Three habits make that concrete:

✗Common mistake

Building the workflow first and treating the two human gates as paperwork to add at the end, or standing in for them yourself. A workflow that only runs in your hands, on your examples, is a performance, not a system, and your own sign-off is not the independent review the scorecard requires. Name your reviewer and your cold-run teammate on day one, and design the adversarial and broken-step runs in from the first draft.

  • Start from the one-sentence job. If you cannot say in a single sentence what the workflow does, with its inputs and outputs, it is too big to defend. Shrink it until you can.
  • Line up your reviewer and teammate before you build, not after. The two mandatory gates depend on another human: an independent reviewer and a teammate who runs it cold. If you build first and look for those people after, you will be tempted to skip them, and skipping them fails the capstone. Name them on day one.
  • Design the failures in, don’t hope they don’t happen. The adversarial run and the broken-step run are not tests you pass by luck, they are features you build. From the first draft, decide where the human gate sits, where the kill switch is, and how you restore. You are building a thing that fails safely, so the safe-failure behavior is part of the design, not an afterthought.

Everything below is the scorecard your reviewer will use. Read it before you start, because it is also your build plan.

The scorecard

◆Show It Works, Show It Fails Safe

Build a small workflow. There are nine scored rows and two mandatory pass/fail gates.

Nine scored rows (0 = missing, 1 = partial, 2 = complete with dated evidence):

  1. Job in one sentence, with inputs and outputs.
  2. Working runs with saved evidence: inputs, outputs, and logs.
  3. Ten case eval (Lesson 20) using the eval sheet mix: five safe, three adversarial, two prohibited. Failing any adversarial or prohibited case is an automatic fail.
  4. Injected failure: run an input that contains a hidden instruction. Show the workflow does not obey it.
  5. Operational failure: break something on purpose (a tool times out, a step fails halfway, a file is missing). Show the workflow stops safely, the log shows it, and you recover from your backup.
  6. Technically enforced approval gate by an authorized person for any risky action. If your workflow has no natural risky action, add a safe simulated one, such as “send” to a test file, and show the gate stops it until approved.
  7. Least privilege access and a reconstructable log.
  8. Kill switch and rollback, demonstrated live.
  9. Written handoff instructions: a written handoff complete enough that a stranger could cold-start and run the workflow from it alone, touching nothing you did not write down.

Two mandatory pass/fail gates (each is pass or fail, not scored; failing either one fails the whole capstone no matter how high the nine rows score):

  • Teammate cold run: a teammate ran it from your written instructions alone, with you not in the room.
  • Independent review: a reviewer who is not you scored the nine rows.

Automatic fail: unauthorized data use, any uncontrolled external action, or failing any adversarial or prohibited case.

💡Bring these templates

The capstone pulls together the whole course, so bring the artifacts you already used instead of starting from scratch:

  • plan-and-gates-template.md: plan the job and where the human gates sit (rows 1, 6)
  • eval-sheet.md: your ten case eval (row 3)
  • tool-intake-checklist.md: vet any tool or connector before you wire it in
  • automation-safety-checklist.md: the safety defenses (rows 4-8)
  • helper-brief-template.md: the written handoff a stranger cold-starts from (row 9 and the teammate gate)

Plan your capstone

Before you build, write these three down:

  1. 1

    The one sentence job, with inputs and outputs.

  2. 2

    Your ten eval cases.

    Mark which three are adversarial and which two are prohibited.
  3. 3

    The name of your reviewer and the name of your teammate.

If you cannot name a reviewer and a teammate, find them before you build: the two mandatory gates depend on them, and they are the hardest to add at the end.

Check yourself

Multiple choice

A learner scores 2 on everything but 1 on the kill switch. What happens?

The capstone is graded on whether the system fails safely, not on an average. A weak kill switch means the workflow cannot be stopped under attack (exactly the failure the project exists to prevent) so a high score everywhere else cannot buy it back. Safety rows are pass/fail, not points to average.

Multiple choice

Your ten-case eval passes all five safe cases but fails one prohibited case. What is the result?

Safe cases show the workflow does its job; adversarial and prohibited cases show it refuses what it must refuse. A failed prohibited case means the system did something it was supposed to block, and that is the whole risk the capstone guards against, so it is an automatic fail no matter how many safe cases passed.

True / False

Because you built the workflow and watched it run, your own sign-off counts as the independent review.

The scorecard requires a reviewer who is not you, plus a teammate who can run the workflow from your instructions alone. Paperwork is not proof, and neither is your own confidence: the whole point of the capstone is evidence someone else can check.

ExerciseWhich of the scorecard items will be hardest for you, and what is your plan?

An honest pick and a concrete plan. For most people it is one of the two mandatory gates (the cold teammate run or the independent review) because those cannot be faked or done solo at the last minute.

Key terms

📖Key terms

Capstone
The final project: build a small AI workflow, prove with saved evidence that it works and that someone else can run it, and show it fails safely.
Injected failure
A run where the input hides an instruction. You pass by showing the workflow does not obey it.
Operational failure
Breaking something on purpose (a tool times out, a step fails halfway, a file is missing) to show the workflow stops safely, logs it, and recovers from backup.
Automatic fail
A result that fails the whole capstone regardless of other scores: unauthorized data use, any uncontrolled external action, or failing any adversarial or prohibited eval case.
Independent review
Scoring by a reviewer who is not you, plus a teammate who can run the workflow from your instructions alone. Your own sign-off does not count.

💡Before you leave

  • State the job in one sentence with inputs and outputs, and save evidence of working runs with logs.
  • Run the ten case eval, five safe, three adversarial, two prohibited, with no adversarial or prohibited case obeyed.
  • Show the injected failure and the operational failure both stop safely, logged, with a recovery from backup.
  • Demonstrate the enforced approval gate, least privilege with a reconstructable log, and a live kill switch and rollback.
  • Clear both mandatory gates: a teammate cold-runs it from your written handoff alone, and an independent reviewer who is not you scores the nine rows.
