ELECTIVES · ELECTIVE 4

Claude with spreadsheets and data

Claude is useful on a spreadsheet only when you give it the column meanings and sample rows, check every formula and number it produces, try on a small slice first, and keep sensitive data out.

ElectivesConcept9 min read

🎯Learning objective

By the end of this lesson you can use Claude on a spreadsheet or dataset by giving it the column meanings and sample rows it needs, checking every formula and number it produces, trying on a small slice first, and keeping sensitive data out.

Why this matters

A spreadsheet looks like the perfect job for Claude. You have a table, you want a formula, a pivot, a cleanup pass, or a plain-English read of what the numbers say, and Claude writes all of that fluently. That fluency is exactly the trap. On prose a wrong sentence often reads a little off. On data a wrong formula returns a number, and a number carries authority: it lands in a report, a decision, a client email, and nobody can tell by looking that the SUM skipped a column or the average quietly dropped the blank rows.

So the problem with data work is not getting Claude to produce something. It produces something every time. The problem is that the output is a computation you have to trust, and Claude cannot see your actual sheet the way you can. It works from what you paste and what you tell it, and it fills the gaps with confident guesses. This lesson is the discipline that makes that safe: feed it the context a data task really needs, then check what it computed before the number leaves your hands.

Where a bare table goes wrong

Start with a concrete pair. Two people hand Claude the same messy sales export and ask for a formula to total revenue per region.

Fiona pastes the raw table with no explanation and asks “give me a formula for total revenue by region.” The columns are headed amt, reg, dt, st. Claude guesses: it reads amt as the revenue column and writes a clean SUMIF. The formula is syntactically perfect. But amt was the quoted amount, not the closed amount; the real revenue lived in a column Claude never saw described. The total is wrong, it looks right, and it ships.

Dev works differently. Before asking for anything he tells Claude what the table is: “amt is the quoted amount, closed is the amount that actually closed, reg is region, st is deal status where only won counts as revenue.” He pastes five sample rows so Claude can see the shape, including one blank closed and one lost deal. Then he asks for the formula. Now Claude has what it needs: it sums closed where st is won, and handles the blank. Dev still does not trust it on sight. He picks one region, adds those rows on a calculator, and reconciles the two numbers. They match, so the formula ships.

The difference is not that Dev used a bigger model or knows more Excel. It is that Dev gave Claude the context the task needed (column meanings, sample rows, what counts) and then checked the computation instead of the prose. Fiona trusted a formula that looked right; Dev trusted a number he reconciled.

✗Common mistake

Trusting a formula because it is well-formed and returns a number. A clean SUMIF can sum the wrong column, silently drop the blank rows, or double-count a join, and a wrong number carries the same authority as a right one straight into a report. Fiona’s total parsed perfectly and was wrong. Recompute one row by hand and reconcile a total against a figure you already trust before the number leaves your hands.

The two moves that make data work safe

Dev’s process is two disciplines, and it is worth seeing why each exists before you memorize the steps.

Give Claude the context a data task needs. A table of bare headers is not enough, because Claude cannot open your sheet and cannot know that amt is quoted and closed is real. It will guess, and a confident guess on data produces a wrong number that looks exactly like a right one. So before you ask for a formula, a pivot, or a cleanup, tell Claude three things: what each column means (especially any header that is abbreviated or ambiguous), a few real sample rows so it can see the actual shape and spot the blanks and odd values, and what the output should look like (a single formula, a new column, a summary table). The sample rows do double duty: they show Claude the edge cases (a blank, a lost row, a stray text value in a number column) that decide whether a formula is correct or just plausible.

Check what it computed, not just that it answered. This is the move people skip, because a formula that parses and a table that renders both feel finished. They are not. A formula can be well-formed and still sum the wrong column, drop blanks you wanted counted, or double-count a join. The only safe check is independent: recompute one row by hand (or on a calculator) and see if Claude’s formula gives the same answer, and reconcile a total against a number you already trust (last month’s figure, a control count, a known subtotal). This is the same answer-checking discipline from checking the answer before you act on it, and when a data workflow runs more than once it graduates into a small eval: a couple of rows with known-correct answers you can re-run to confirm the formula still holds after a change.

Two habits ride along with those moves. Try it on a small slice first: run the formula or transformation on ten rows before ten thousand, so a mistake costs a few rows you can eyeball, not a whole sheet you have to untangle. And keep sensitive data out: a spreadsheet is where personal and confidential data hides (salaries, client names, health or deal details), so strip, mask, or aggregate anything your data rules do not allow before you paste or upload, the same rule the public and rollout electives lean on, applied to tables. Claude cannot un-see what you give it.

The rule

◆Context In, Check the Numbers Out

  1. Give context: what each column means, a few real sample rows, and what the output should look like.
  2. Keep sensitive data out: strip or mask anything your data rules do not allow before you paste or upload.
  3. Ask for one clear thing: a formula, a new column, a pivot, or a plain-English read.
  4. Try a small slice first: run it on ten rows before the whole sheet.
  5. Check the computation: recompute one row by hand and reconcile a total against a number you already trust.
  6. Re-runnable work gets an eval: keep a couple of known-answer rows to re-check the formula after any change.

Try it

You asked Claude for help on a sales sheet. For each result below, decide: does it clear, yes or no, and if no, name the exact fix. Judge whether Claude had the context it needed and whether the computation was checked, not whether the formula merely looks valid. Write your four calls in the box below before you reveal.

  1. You pasted headers amt, reg, st with no explanation and got a SUMIF on amt. You ship it as “total revenue.”
  2. You explained every column and pasted five sample rows, then recomputed one region by hand and it matched the formula.
  3. You ran the new cleanup formula on all 40,000 rows first, to save time, then spot-checked afterward.
  4. The sheet has a salary column your data rules forbid sharing. You pasted the whole sheet so Claude “has the full picture.”
ExerciseReveal the answers
  1. No. Context fails: bare abbreviated headers let Claude guess what amt is, and “quoted” versus “closed” is exactly the guess that goes wrong. Fix: tell Claude what each column means and which status counts as revenue, then check the total.
  2. Yes. Claude had column meanings and sample rows, and you reconciled the number independently. The formula clears (keep a known-answer row if you will re-run it).
  3. No, not in that order. Small slice first: running on all 40,000 rows before checking means a wrong formula corrupts the whole sheet. Fix: run on ten rows, verify, then scale.
  4. No. Sensitive data: a forbidden salary column does not belong in the paste. Fix: strip or mask the salary column before uploading; “full picture” never overrides your data rules.

Check yourself

Multiple choice

Before asking Claude for a formula on a table, what should you give it?

Claude cannot open your sheet, so a bare header lets it guess what each column is. Column meanings plus real sample rows plus the output shape give it what it needs; a bigger model still guesses on missing context, and dumping the whole database adds risk, not clarity.

Multiple choice

Claude returns a well-formed formula that totals revenue. What do you do before you trust the number?

A formula can parse and still sum the wrong column or drop blanks. The only safe check is independent: recompute a row and reconcile against a known number. 'It parses' is exactly how Fiona's wrong total shipped, and another model just produces more confident output, not a check.

True / False

A spreadsheet formula that runs without an error is correct.

Running without an error only means the formula is well-formed, not that it computes the right thing. It can sum the quoted column instead of the closed one, silently drop blank rows, or double-count a join, and return a clean number every time. Correctness is a separate question you answer by recomputing a row and reconciling a total, not by the absence of an error.

Key terms

📖Key terms

Column context
Telling Claude what each column means (especially abbreviated or ambiguous headers), since it cannot open your sheet and will otherwise guess, and a confident wrong guess on data looks exactly like a right one.
Sample rows
A few real rows pasted so Claude sees the actual shape and the edge cases (blanks, odd values, status rows) that decide whether a formula is correct or merely plausible.
Check the computation
Verifying what Claude computed, not just that it answered: recompute one row by hand and reconcile a total against a number you already trust, because a well-formed formula can still sum the wrong thing.
Small slice first
Running a new formula or transformation on a handful of rows before the whole sheet, so a mistake costs a few rows you can eyeball rather than corrupting everything.
Data rules on a sheet
The limits on what information may go into the tool, applied to spreadsheets where sensitive data hides in columns; strip, mask, or aggregate anything forbidden before you paste or upload.

💡Before you leave

  • Explain why a bare table misleads Claude: without column meanings and sample rows it guesses what the columns are, and a confident wrong formula looks exactly like a right one.
  • Give Claude the context a data task needs: what each column means, a few real sample rows, and what the output should look like.
  • Check what Claude computed, not just that it answered: recompute one row by hand and reconcile a total before you trust the formula or the number.
  • Try a formula or transformation on a small slice first, so a mistake costs a few rows and not the whole sheet.
  • Keep sensitive data out: strip or mask what your data rules do not allow before anything is pasted or uploaded.
