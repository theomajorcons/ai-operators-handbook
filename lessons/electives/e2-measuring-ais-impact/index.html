ELECTIVES · ELECTIVE 2

Measuring AI's impact

An honest AI saving counts the review and cleanup time, not just the fast AI run, then turns the net weekly saving into a payback period kept in one unit.

ElectivesConcept8 min read

🎯Learning objective

By the end of this lesson you can show honestly whether an AI workflow saves time or money (counting the review and cleanup, not just the AI run) and compute a payback period.

Why this matters

Sooner or later someone asks the question: is the AI actually worth it? Your manager, your team, or just yourself deciding whether to keep a workflow. And the honest answer is almost never the one that first comes to mind, because the AI run is the part you see (it’s fast and impressive) while the costs that eat the savings are the parts you don’t count: the time spent reviewing the output, the time fixing what it got wrong, and the hours you spent setting the whole thing up.

A claim like “AI saved us tons of time” that ignores those costs isn’t a measurement, it’s a feeling. This lesson shows you how to turn the feeling into a number you could defend: a real before-and-after that counts the cleanup, and a payback period that tells you when the setup pays for itself.

The hidden cost that eats the savings

Here’s the trap, with numbers. Two people run the same task with Claude’s help.

Ned says AI saved his team “tons of time.” Ask him how much and he has nothing, just the impression that things feel faster. That feeling is real, but it’s unverifiable, and it’s usually inflated by the vivid memory of the fast AI run.

Oli actually timed it. The task used to take 40 minutes by hand. With Claude, the AI run itself took just 5 minutes, which is the number Ned would have quoted and felt great about. But Oli kept timing: reviewing the output and fixing its mistakes took 30 more minutes. So the real after-time is 35 minutes, not 5. The genuine saving is 5 minutes per run, not 35.

That gap (5 minutes saved versus 35 minutes imagined) is the whole lesson. The review and cleanup time is invisible when you tell the story but decisive when you do the math.

✗Common mistake

Counting the visible AI run as the saving. The 5-minute run is the part you remember, so “it saved 35 minutes” feels true, but the real after-time is 35 once you add the review and the fixes, and the honest saving is 5. The cleanup is invisible when you tell the story and decisive when you do the math. Always time the whole task with AI, not just the run.

Turning the saving into a decision

A per-run saving still isn’t the full picture, because setting up the workflow cost something too. To decide whether it was worth it, you compare the one-time setup cost against what it earns back each week. This is the payback period, and the trap to avoid is mixing units: you can measure payback in time or in money, but not by dividing one by the other.

Time payback is the pure-time version, and it is net: setup hours divided by the hours you net save per week, after subtracting the ongoing upkeep hours. That subtraction matters because the per-run saving is not the only thing that moves. Reviewing and fixing each run is already inside Oli’s 35-minute after-time, so the 5-minute per-run saving has that cleanup baked in. But keeping a workflow honest costs a second, separate kind of time: tuning prompts, re-running evals, fixing drift. That upkeep is a recurring weekly cost, not a per-run one, so it has to come off the weekly saving on its own.

Keep going with Oli’s numbers. He saves 5 minutes per run and runs the task 10 times a week, so the gross saving is 50 minutes per week. But he also spends about 10 minutes a week on upkeep (a prompt tweak here, a quick re-eval there). Subtract it and the net weekly saving is 40 minutes. The setup took him 4 hours (240 minutes) to build. So the net time payback is 240 ÷ 40 = 6 weeks, not the 5 weeks you would get from the gross number. Both sides are time, so the answer is clean: after about six weeks of use the setup hours have been earned back in net saved hours. One thing you do not subtract again here is the per-run review, it is already inside the after-time, so counting it a second time would double-charge the cleanup. The only thing left out of this calculation is the tool subscription (a flat dollar cost), which belongs in the financial payback instead, where everything is converted to one currency.

⚠Warning

If the weekly upkeep ever grows to equal or exceed the weekly gross saving, the net saving is zero or negative and there is no time payback at all, the setup never earns itself back in time. Watch that number.

Financial payback is the money version, and it is where the ongoing costs live. It is setup cost in dollars divided by net dollars saved per week, where “net” means you have already subtracted the ongoing costs from the weekly saving: the flat tool cost in dollars, plus the maintenance, re-eval, and review hours converted to dollars at your hourly rate. (That conversion is the key move: upkeep is labor you count in hours first, then monetize at a rate so it lands in the same currency as everything else.) The impact tracker below spells out that money math: value the saved time at an hourly rate, add any real cash savings, then subtract the weekly tool cost and the monetized upkeep hours to get net benefit per week. Because the ongoing costs shrink the weekly net, financial payback is usually longer than time payback, and if the ongoing costs ever exceed the weekly saving there is no payback at all, the workflow loses money every week.

So the two numbers answer two honest questions. Time payback asks “when do I get my setup hours back?” Financial payback asks “when does this pay for itself in money, after what it costs to keep running?” Keep each one in its own unit and neither one lies.

To get an honest payback you need five ingredients, and the first discipline is counting all the costs, not just the visible AI run:

  • Baseline: time the task before AI, over several runs, so your starting number is real and not a guess.
  • After: time it with AI, and crucially include the review and the fixes, because that’s where the minutes hide.
  • Quality: record the error rate or rework count too; a workflow that’s faster but wronger isn’t a win.
  • Cost: the ongoing tool and maintenance cost (dollars), plus the one-time setup you’re trying to earn back.
  • Payback: time payback is setup hours ÷ net hours saved per week (gross weekly saving minus the weekly upkeep hours); financial payback is setup cost ÷ net dollars saved per week (ongoing costs already subtracted). Either one has no payback if the ongoing cost wipes out the weekly saving.

The rule

◆Baseline, Compare, Count the Cleanup

  1. Baseline: time the task before AI, over several runs.
  2. After: time it with AI, including review and fixes.
  3. Quality: record error rate or rework count too.
  4. Cost: ongoing tool and maintenance cost (dollars), plus one-time setup.
  5. Payback: time = setup hours ÷ net hours saved per week (gross saving minus weekly upkeep hours); financial = setup cost ÷ net dollars saved per week (ongoing costs subtracted). No payback either way if upkeep wipes out the saving.

Try it

Compute the net time payback (all time, no dollars). Write your three numbers (saving per run, net saving per week, payback) in the box below before you reveal.

Setup takes 12 hours. The task was 20 minutes by hand, now 8 minutes including review. You run it 15 times a week, and weekly upkeep (prompt tweaks, re-evals) runs about 1 hour.

ExerciseReveal the answers

Saving per run: 20 − 8 = 12 minutes (the review is already inside the 8-minute after-time, so don’t subtract it again). Gross per week: 12 × 15 = 180 minutes, or 3 hours. Now subtract the 1 hour of weekly upkeep: net saving is 2 hours per week. Net time payback: 12 hours ÷ 2 hours per week = 6 weeks. Both sides are hours, so the units line up; the dollar tool cost would go into the separate financial payback, not here.

Check yourself

Multiple choice

What belongs in the after time?

The after-time is the whole task with AI: run plus review plus fixes. Counting only the AI run is exactly Ned's mistake: Oli's run was 5 minutes but the honest after-time was 35.

True / False

Ned's claim that AI saved 'tons of time' is credible because the team feels faster.

Feeling faster is not a measurement. Oli’s claim is credible because he baselined the task before, timed it after including review and fixes, and could show the saving per run. A feeling with no numbers can’t be checked, and the vivid, fast AI run usually makes the feeling bigger than the real saving.

Multiple choice

Setup is 6 hours. You save 3 hours a week gross, but 1 hour a week goes to upkeep (prompt tweaks, re-evals). What is the net time payback period?

Net time payback is setup hours ÷ net hours saved per week. Net saving is 3 − 1 = 2 hours per week, so 6 ÷ 2 = 3 weeks. Subtract the weekly upkeep from the gross saving first; the per-run review is already inside the after-time and is not subtracted again.

Key terms

📖Key terms

Baseline
The time a task takes before AI, measured over several runs so you have an honest starting number to compare against.
After time
The time the task takes with AI: the run plus review and fixes, not the AI run alone.
Time payback
Setup hours divided by net hours saved per week, where net is the gross weekly saving minus the weekly upkeep hours (prompt tuning, re-evals). Pure time; the per-run review is already inside the after-time so it is not subtracted again, and ongoing dollar costs belong in financial payback. No payback if upkeep wipes out the saving.
Financial payback
Setup cost divided by net dollars saved per week, where the weekly saving already has the ongoing tool, maintenance, and review costs subtracted. If ongoing costs exceed the saving, there is no payback.
Cleanup cost
The review and rework time after an AI run; omitting it is the most common way ROI claims get inflated.

💡Before you leave

  • Explain why the visible AI run overstates the saving: it leaves out review time, fix time, and setup hours.
  • Compute a real per-run saving from a baseline time and an after-time that includes review and fixes.
  • Record quality too (error rate or rework count), since a workflow that is faster but wronger is not a win.
  • State the difference between gross and net weekly saving: net subtracts the recurring weekly upkeep hours.
  • Compute time payback (setup hours over net hours saved per week) and financial payback (setup cost over net dollars saved per week) without mixing units.
  • Say when there is no payback at all: when ongoing upkeep equals or exceeds the weekly saving.
