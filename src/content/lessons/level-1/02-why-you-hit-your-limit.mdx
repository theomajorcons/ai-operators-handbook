## Why you run out sooner than you expect

Plenty of people hit their usage limit on days they did not feel like they asked for much: a handful of short questions, and suddenly the meter is empty. It feels unfair, because it looks like you barely used anything. The cause is almost never the questions you *see*. It is the pile of material each question quietly drags along behind it.

This lesson teaches you where that hidden cost comes from, so you can stop paying it.

## The two kinds of limit

First, clear up a confusion that trips almost everyone, because "limit" means two different things.

- **Your usage limit** is your overall allowance *over time*, set by your plan or subscription. Think of it as a tank that refills on a schedule (a session window, a weekly cap). When the tank is empty you wait for it to refill. You do not control the size of the tank; your plan does. You only control how fast you drain it.
- **A chat getting too long** is a *single conversation* growing so large that it becomes slow, expensive per turn, and forgetful. This one you control completely, and it is a major thing draining the tank faster than it should.

Keep these two straight, because they are easy to blur. Your plan's usage cap is *how much you are allotted* before you have to wait. A chat's context filling up is a *different thing*: it's the running history getting so big that answers slow down, cost more, and lose track of earlier detail, a quality problem inside one conversation, not your overall allowance running out. Hitting a wall can be either one, and the fix differs: a full allowance means waiting for the refill (or a bigger plan), while a bloated chat means starting fresh.

The rest of this lesson is about the second kind, because fixing it is the lever that keeps the first kind from biting.

## What a chat actually carries

Here is the piece most people never get told. When you send a message in a chat, Claude does not just read that message. It reads the **context** (the running history of the whole conversation, plus everything you have attached) and your new message rides on top of all of it. The [next lesson](/lessons/level-1/03-give-claude-the-right-context/) covers everything context includes; here it is enough to know it is cumulative.

That history is cumulative. Turn one carries a little. Turn forty carries turn one, turn two, and the thirty-eight turns since, *plus* every file you uploaded along the way. The question you type at turn forty might be three words long, but Claude is working against the entire stack behind it.

<ContextStack />

Growing context isn't the only thing that can empty your allowance: a single oversized attachment, or simply doing a lot in a short window against a plan's usage caps, can do it too. But for the "I barely asked anything" surprise, a long, heavy chat is usually the main driver, and it's the one you control most directly, so it's what the rest of this lesson targets:

- **Cost.** Each turn's price scales with how much context it has to carry. A short question on top of a huge conversation is an expensive turn, because the *conversation* is what is big, not your question. More context in view means more of your allowance spent per turn, even when the new question is tiny.
- **Quality.** A large pile of old material also makes the *relevant* detail harder to surface. Claude can lose an earlier fact, repeat itself, or lean on an assumption you already corrected twenty turns ago, simply because the signal is buried in noise.

### A worked example

Picture **Priya** planning a trip. She uploads a flight confirmation, a hotel booking, a packing list, and two long articles, all into one chat. Then she asks a question. Then another. Then thirty more, over two days, in that same chat. By the afternoon she hits her limit, and Claude starts missing details she gave it on day one.

Her questions were short. So what drained her? The chat itself. Every one of those thirty turns carried all five documents and the entire growing history forward. The two failures arrived together: the **cost** climbed with each turn, and the **quality** dropped as day-one details got buried. That is the long-chat problem in one picture.

<Callout variant="mistake" label="Common mistake">

Behind the scenes, Claude may cache, summarize, or retrieve only the parts of a long chat that look relevant, so it is not literally re-reading every word on every turn. That management helps, but it does not make a long chat free. A focused, shorter context still costs less and gives sharper answers. Do not count on the automatic handling to rescue a chat that has sprawled.

</Callout>

## The rule

When a chat gets long, do not keep pushing on it. Start a fresh one and carry forward only what matters.

<Callout variant="framework" label="Fresh Chat, Focused Handoff">

1. **Notice the signs.** Claude starts missing earlier details, repeats itself, or relies on something you already corrected. Hitting limits sooner than usual is a second signal.
2. **Ask Claude to write the handoff.** "Summarize what we decided, what is still open, the key facts and numbers I will need, and which files matter."
3. **Check the handoff.** Read it and correct anything missing or wrong *before* you leave the original chat.
4. **Open a new chat.** Paste the handoff and attach only the files you still need.

A fresh chat reduces the context carried forward, so each turn gets cheaper and sharper. It does **not** reset your session or weekly usage limit; it just stops you from draining that limit faster than the task deserves.

</Callout>

## Try it: keep or restart?

For each situation, decide: keep going, or restart with a handoff? **Write your three answers down before you reveal.**

1. A 6-message chat to reword one paragraph.
2. A 60-message chat planning a trip, with five attached files, and Claude keeps forgetting the budget you set on day one.
3. A new topic that has nothing to do with the earlier chat.

<ExerciseReveal prompt="Reveal the answers" commit="Write keep-or-restart for all three, then reveal:">
<Fragment slot="answer">

1. **Keep going.** It is short: the context is tiny, so the cost is tiny. Restarting would cost more effort than it saves.
2. **Restart with a handoff.** Long, heavy with attachments, and Claude is already losing day-one details. Both failure signals are firing.
3. **Restart, no handoff needed.** A new topic gets a new chat. Dragging the old material in only adds weight for nothing.

</Fragment>
</ExerciseReveal>
