<Callout variant="objective" label="Learning objective">

By the end of this lesson you can take an answer from Claude, find the claims it can be checked against, verify the few the task leans on, and decide whether to use it.

</Callout>

## Why this matters

You have learned to pick a model, keep a chat lean, hand over the right context, and [write a prompt like a spec](/lessons/level-1/04-write-a-prompt-like-a-spec/). All of that gets you a better answer. This lesson is about the step most people skip: deciding whether to trust the answer you got. It is the other half of the Check you wrote into your spec, done after the answer comes back.

Here is the trap. Claude writes with the same calm confidence whether it is right or wrong. A correct total and a wrong total come back in the same tidy sentence, in the same sure voice. The fluency is not evidence. It tells you the writing is good, not that the facts are. So the habit to build is simple: before an answer leaves your hands, check the parts the task leans on.

You do not re-grade the whole thing. You find the claims that can be checked and verify the ones that carry weight.

## Two people, one answer

Claude returns the same report to two interns. It summarizes a vendor quote and ends: "The total is 12,400 dollars, the contract renews on March 3, 2026, and Acme is the only vendor offering net-60 terms."

**Dana** reads it, nods, and pastes the numbers straight into an email to her manager. It reads well and it sounds sure, so she treats sounding sure as being sure. The quote actually totaled 14,200 dollars; Claude transposed two digits. Dana's email now carries a wrong number in her own name, and the confident tone is exactly what kept her from looking twice.

**Priya** reads the same report and first marks the **checkable claims**: a total (12,400), a date (March 3), and a named fact (only vendor with net-60). Three claims she could verify, as opposed to the wording around them. Then she asks which ones the decision rests on. The total drives the budget ask, so that is **load-bearing**; she opens the quote PDF and adds the line items herself. They come to 14,200. She has caught the error before it left the building, and she fixes it rather than forwarding it.

Same answer, same confident voice. Dana trusted the tone; Priya checked the claims that mattered. That is the whole difference.

<Callout variant="mistake" label="Common mistake">

Reading a sure, fluent answer as a correct one. Claude writes a wrong total in the same tidy, confident sentence as a right one, so the smoothness tells you nothing about the facts. The trap is letting that tone stand in for a check. Find the load-bearing claim and verify it before the answer leaves your hands.

</Callout>

## The rule

<Callout variant="framework" label="Find, Pick, Verify">

1. **Find the checkable claims.** Scan the answer for anything concrete: dates, numbers, named facts, quotes, specific steps. These are things that are either true or not, as opposed to tone or phrasing.
2. **Pick the load-bearing ones.** Ask which claims the decision or the next action rests on. You verify those, not every line. A wrong adjective is cosmetic; a wrong total is not.
3. **Verify independently.** Go to the source, recompute the number, or cross-check a second place. Asking Claude "are you sure?" is not a check: it can restate a wrong answer just as confidently.

Then **decide**: use it, fix the parts that failed, or drop it and ask again with better context.

</Callout>

A fast way in: ask yourself "what in here would embarrass me if it were wrong?" That question finds the load-bearing claims almost every time.

This is the same instinct behind two failure signals from [Lesson 2](/lessons/level-1/01-pick-the-right-model/): a missed contradiction and a number that does not add up are things you only catch by checking a claim, not by rereading the tone. And when you do this at scale, as a repeatable set of test cases instead of a one-off read, it grows into an **eval**, the discipline [Lesson 20](/lessons/level-3/17-verification-and-evals/) and the [public-workflows elective](/lessons/electives/e1-public-workflows-research-and-publishing/) build on.

## Try it

For each answer from Claude, name the one claim you would verify first and how you would check it. **Commit to all three before you reveal.**

1. "Based on the attached CSV, total Q3 revenue was 318,000 dollars."
2. "The deadline to file is April 15, and late filings incur a 5 percent monthly penalty."
3. "This function is safe to delete; nothing else in the codebase calls it."

<ExerciseReveal prompt="Reveal the answers" commit="Name your claim and check for all three, then reveal:">
<Fragment slot="answer">

1. **The 318,000 total.** Recompute it from the CSV yourself, or spot-check a few rows that should sum to it. The number drives a decision, so it is load-bearing.
2. **The April 15 date and the 5 percent figure.** Both are checkable facts with consequences; confirm them against the actual filing rule, not against Claude. A wrong date here is expensive.
3. **The "nothing else calls it" claim.** Search the codebase for the function name yourself. This is the load-bearing claim, and the only safe check is independent: deleting on Claude's word is how you break something.

</Fragment>
</ExerciseReveal>
