<Callout variant="objective" label="Learning objective">

By the end of this lesson you can ask for the right **shape** of output for a task (chat reply, table, document, or page) and reshape one result into several formats as your audience changes.

</Callout>

## Why this matters

By default, Claude answers in prose: paragraphs in the chat window. Prose is great for explaining something to *you*, right now. But most work doesn't stop at the chat window: you paste numbers into a spreadsheet, hand a one-pager to a teammate, or build something other people will click on. When the output's shape doesn't match what you'll do with it next, you end up doing the reshaping by hand.

The good news: you can often ask for the output in a specific shape rather than accepting the default. You just have to name it, and that's a small habit worth building, because it saves the copy-paste-reformat step most of the time. (Some shapes suit some content better than others, and a complex conversion may involve trade-offs: a Markdown table in chat is not the same artifact as a downloaded spreadsheet file, and reshaping can occasionally lose structure or nuance. Check the result.)

## The four shapes, and what each is for

Claude can produce its answer in several forms. The trick isn't learning fancy formatting; it's matching the form to the *next thing you'll do with the answer*. Four shapes cover almost everything:

- **A chat reply (prose).** Plain paragraphs in the conversation. Best when you just need to read it once and move on: an explanation, a quick idea, a yes/no with reasoning. It's the default because it's the most flexible, but it's the worst shape for numbers you'll compare.
- **A table or spreadsheet.** Rows and columns. The right call the moment you're comparing several items across the same attributes, or you need totals. A table lines everything up so differences jump out, and it often pastes into a spreadsheet without much friction. Note the distinction: a **Markdown table** rendered in chat is not the same thing as a downloadable **.csv file** or a full spreadsheet workbook; ask for the specific form you need. Asking for prose when you wanted a table is the most common mismatch there is.
- **A document.** A longer, structured piece you'll keep, edit, or share: a report, a memo, a handout. Reach for this when the output has a life beyond the chat: you'll revisit it, revise it, or hand it to someone.
- **A page or small tool.** Something interactive that *other people* will use: a simple calculator, a form, a small web page. This is the step up from "a thing I read" to "a thing others operate." (Exactly what's available here depends on your plan and the features you've turned on.)

### The mismatch in the wild

Here's the cost of not naming the shape. **Eli** asks Claude for a weekly budget and gets four tidy paragraphs of prose. The numbers are fine, but to actually *use* them he retypes each one into a spreadsheet by hand. His roommate **Mina** asks the same question as "give me a table with a column for each category and a totals row," and pastes the result straight into her spreadsheet in one move. Same content, same model; the only difference is that Mina named the shape she'd use next. Eli's answer wasn't wrong; it was just the wrong shape for the job.

<Callout variant="mistake" label="Common mistake">

Accepting the default prose answer and then reformatting it by hand. Eli retyped four paragraphs of budget into a spreadsheet cell by cell, work he never needed to do. If the next thing you will do with an answer is compare, calculate, keep, or share it, name that shape up front and let Claude hand you the form you actually want.

</Callout>

### Shape and correctness are two different things

One trap to flag before the rule: a beautifully formatted table or a polished document can still be *wrong*. A clean table with a wrong total is still a wrong total: the formatting doesn't check the math. So naming the shape gets you a usable form; it doesn't get you a correct answer. That's still your job: glance at the contents before you send or save.

## The rule

<Callout variant="framework" label="Name the Shape">

1. **Read once?** A reply in the chat.
2. **Compare or calculate?** A table or spreadsheet.
3. **Keep, edit, or share?** A document.
4. **Others will use it?** A page or small tool.
5. **Check the contents** before you send or save it.

Available output types depend on your plan and the features you have turned on.

</Callout>

## Try it

Reshape one result four ways and watch the content stay put while the shape changes.

1. Ask Claude: "List five pros and cons of working from home."
2. Ask it to turn that into a two-column table.
3. Ask it to turn the table into a one-page handout for a team meeting.
4. Ask it to turn the handout into three sentences for a text message.
5. Compare the four versions. Which fits which audience?

The same content served four audiences: prose to learn, a table to compare, a handout to share, three sentences to send. Notice you never re-asked the *question*; you only re-named the *shape*. That's the whole skill: decide what you'll do with the answer next, then ask for that form from the start instead of reformatting by hand. Some conversions work better than others: dense structured data reshapes cleanly, but highly formatted documents can lose detail when squeezed into three sentences. Check the result each time.
