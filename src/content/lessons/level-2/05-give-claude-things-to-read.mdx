<Callout variant="objective" label="Learning objective">

By the end of this lesson you can share a document or image with Claude safely, and ask questions that are actually answered from it, not from a vague skim.

</Callout>

## Why this matters

Up to now you have been typing everything Claude works from. But most real work already lives in a file: a report, a contract, a screenshot, a scanned form. The moment you can hand Claude a document and ask about *it*, the tool stops being a clever writer and starts being useful on your actual stuff.

Two things make or break that handoff: **asking in a way that gets you a checkable answer**, and **not leaking private information in the process.** This lesson teaches both before it asks you to try them.

## What Claude can and can't do with a document

Claude can read **pasted text, PDFs, and images** (including photos and scans). When you attach one, its contents join the context from Lesson 4. Claude can now quote it, summarize it, and answer questions from it.

But "can read" is not "has memorized every word." With a long document, two limits bite:

- **A broad question gets a broad answer.** Ask "summarize this" and Claude produces a reasonable-sounding overview, which can quietly skip the one table or clause you actually cared about. It is not lying; it just guessed at what mattered, and guessed wrong.
- **Reading a scan is not perfect.** A column in an image, a smudged number, a table split across a page: these get misread sometimes. So an answer pulled from a document still needs a glance at the source.

Here is the first failure in the wild. **Noor** drops a 30-page PDF into Claude and types "Summarize." She gets a tidy page that sounds right, and later finds it skipped the pricing table she cared about. The fix isn't a better model; it's a narrower question. Had she asked "What does the pricing table on page 12 say? Quote it," she'd have gotten the exact thing she needed *and* a quote she could check against the page.

### The part people forget: what you're handing over

A document is not just information for Claude; it's information **you are sharing.** And documents carry passengers: a pay stub has your income and an account number, a lease has your address, a work export may have a colleague's details or something your employer treats as confidential.

The second failure: **Ben** pastes a scan of his own pay stub to ask a tax question, without thinking about what else is on it. His question was about one number; he shared twenty. The habit to build is a short pause before every attach: *what's on this page that the question doesn't need?* Remove those bits first. That deliberate removal has a name (**redaction**), and it's the cheapest safety step you have. Work documents get a second check: your organization's own rules, which vary by account and plan.

<Callout variant="warning" label="Warning">

Attaching a document shares everything on it, not just the part your question is about. Ben's pay-stub question was about one number; the file also carried his income and account number. Before you attach anything with real personal, customer, or confidential data, pause and strip what the question does not need, on a copy. A file you cannot un-share is a one-way door.

</Callout>

Redaction done wrong is worse than none, because it looks safe while it isn't. Black out a line in a PDF and the original text can still sit underneath the box; "delete" a column and it may survive in the file's hidden data. So redact on a **copy**, never the original, and treat anything hidden as still present: tracked changes, comments, document properties and other **metadata**, cropped-off image regions. For stubborn cases, export to a flattened format (print to a fresh PDF, or export an image) so the removed content can't be recovered. Then the step everyone skips: **reopen the saved copy, search for what you removed, and try to copy the "redacted" area.** If the search finds nothing and the copy comes back empty, it's gone; if either turns up the text, your redaction didn't take.

## The rule

Put those two halves together and you get a three-step routine for every document you share.

<Callout variant="framework" label="Ask Narrow, Check the Page, Share Less">

1. **Ask narrow.** Point at the part you need: "What does the pricing table on page 12 say?", not "summarize this."
2. **Check the page.** Ask Claude to quote or cite where the answer came from, then open the source and confirm it matches.
3. **Share less, and redact safely.** Before attaching, remove anything private the question doesn't need: account numbers, IDs, addresses, other people's details. Do it on a **copy**; remove hidden text, comments, and metadata, not just what's visible; export or flatten if needed; then reopen, search, and copy-test to confirm it's actually gone.

For work documents, also check your organization's rules before sharing: settings and policies differ by account.

</Callout>

## Try it

Practice the redaction on a **synthetic or public** document, never a real sensitive one: make a throwaway file with a few fake names, numbers, and addresses, or use a public report. The point is to rehearse the process safely, so a real file isn't where you discover your redaction leaks.

1. Open `document-sharing-checklist.md` and write down what you'll remove (the fake names, numbers, addresses you planted).
2. Working on a **copy**, redact those items, including hidden text, comments, and document properties, then export or flatten if the format allows.
3. **Reopen the copy, search for what you removed, and try to copy the redacted area.** Confirm it's actually gone before going further.
4. Attach the cleaned copy, ask one narrow question, and request a quote: "What does page X say about Y? Quote it."
5. Open the page yourself, compare the quote, and fill in one row of the log.

If the quote doesn't match the page, you just found why *Check the page* matters: that mismatch is the whole point of the step. If you have no document handy, use a public one such as a product manual. The skill you're building is the pause before attaching and the glance after answering.
