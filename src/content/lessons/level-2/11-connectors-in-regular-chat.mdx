By the end of this lesson you can evaluate whether to connect Claude to one of your apps, plan the minimum access it would need, and decide exactly what it should be allowed to **see** and **do**.

</Callout>

## Why this matters

So far, Claude only knows what you type or attach. A **connector** changes that: it links Claude directly to one of your apps (your email, your calendar, your files) so it can pull information from it, and sometimes act on it, without you copying anything in. That's genuinely useful: "summarize today's calendar" stops being a paste job and becomes one sentence.

But every connector you add widens the circle of what Claude can reach, and the same link that lets it *read* your calendar might also let it *change* it. So the skill isn't "turn on connectors." It's deciding, before you connect anything, the smallest amount of access that still does the job. This lesson teaches that decision before it asks you to make one.

## What a connector actually grants

Two things about a connector decide how much you're handing over, and they're worth separating:

- **Scope: how much it can reach.** Connecting one app (just your calendar) is a narrow scope. Connecting email *and* calendar *and* files at once is a wide one. The wider the scope, the more of your life Claude can see, and the more there is to go wrong if a request goes sideways. A connector set up for "everything" sees everything, even when today's job only needed one thing. (What connectors are available varies by account, organization, and administrator settings; check what your plan and admin allow before planning a connection.)
- **Permission: read, or read *and* write.** Reading is Claude looking at your data. Writing is Claude *changing* it: sending the email, moving the meeting, deleting the file. Many connectors let you grant read-only access, which is a very different risk from letting Claude act on your behalf. When a job only needs Claude to *look*, read-only is the safer grant, full stop. (Which permission options exist, and what the consent screen shows, varies by connector and plan.)

Put those together and the safe default is obvious: the fewest apps, with the least power, that still does the one thing you actually want.

<Callout variant="warning" label="Warning">

Write access lets Claude act for you: send the email, move the meeting, delete the file. Those are one-way doors, and a connector granted write access can walk through them on a misread request. Grant read-only whenever the job only needs Claude to look, and if a connector must act, review every send or change before it goes through.

</Callout>

### The contrast in the wild

**Yuki** connects her email, calendar, *and* files all at once so Claude can "help with everything." It's convenient, until she realizes how much it can now see and act on, far beyond anything she's actually asked it to do. Her friend **Marcus** connects only his calendar, read-only, for a single job: a Monday summary of his week. Marcus gave up nothing he didn't need to. Yuki opened wide for a vague "everything," and a wide, powerful connection is exactly the thing that turns a small mistake into a big one. Neither of them did anything exotic; the only difference is that Marcus named the job first and let it set the access.

## The rule

<Callout variant="framework" label="One Job, Least Access">

1. **Name the job** before you connect anything.
2. **Connect the minimum** that does it.
3. **Read before write.** Choose read-only access when the connector offers it. Permission options vary by connector and plan.
4. **Review actions.** If Claude can send or change things, check before it does.
5. **Disconnect** what you no longer use.

Options differ by account and organization, so check what yours allow.

</Callout>

## Try it

Evaluate a connector without connecting anything; the plan is the whole exercise. Pick one real app you use. (Whether this connection is available to you depends on your plan, account, and any administrator restrictions; the goal here is the decision-making practice, not completing the connection itself.)

1. Open `connector-decision-sheet.md`.
2. Write the one job (for example, "summarize my calendar each Monday").
3. Write the minimum access it needs, and whether read-only is available.
4. Write who approves any action, and the date you would disconnect it.
5. Only then decide whether to connect it.
