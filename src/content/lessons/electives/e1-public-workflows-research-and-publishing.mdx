<Callout variant="objective" label="Learning objective">

By the end of this lesson you can run a research and publishing workflow where every material factual claim traces to a source you opened and vetted, and a person reviews the draft before it goes live.

</Callout>

## Why this matters

The moment your Claude-assisted work goes *public* (a blog post, a report, a page with your name on it), the rules change. A private draft that's a little wrong costs you a second look. A published page that's a little wrong costs you your credibility, and it does it in front of everyone, permanently.

The danger is specific: Claude writes fluent, confident prose whether or not the facts behind it are real. A made-up date reads exactly like a checked one. So the whole problem of public work is separating the claims that happen to be true from the claims you've actually *verified*, and never letting the unverified ones ship. This lesson gives you a workflow that does that by construction.

## Where unsourced work goes wrong

Start with a concrete pair. Two people write the same kind of post (a short history of a local park), both with Claude's help.

**Gwen** asks Claude to write it, reads it over, and publishes. It reads well. Then a reader points out two claims with no source behind them and one that's flat wrong: the park opened in 1935, not the 1925 her post states. The error was invisible to her because the sentence looked just as confident as every true sentence around it.

**Hal** works differently. Before drafting, he collects the pages he'll rely on. As he writes, he marks which claim came from which page. For the key facts (dates, numbers, names) he actually opens the source and checks. His line reads: "The park opened in 1935 (city archive page, opened and checked Oct 12)." When a reader pushes back, he has the source ready.

The difference isn't that Hal is a better writer or used a bigger model. It's that Hal built a **process** Gwen didn't: he grounded each factual claim in a source he opened, kept the list, and treated publishing as a gate a person passes the work through, not a button Claude presses.

<Callout variant="mistake" label="Common mistake">

Publishing because the draft reads well. Claude writes a made-up date in exactly the same confident sentence as a checked one, so "it reads right" tells you nothing about whether the facts are real. Gwen's wrong year looked as sure as every true line around it. Trace every material claim to a source you actually opened before it ships, not after a reader catches it.

</Callout>

## The two moves that make public work safe

Hal's process is really two disciplines, and it's worth seeing *why* each one exists before you memorize the steps.

**Source every material factual claim.** This does not mean a footnote on every sentence. It means every *material factual claim*: the dates, numbers, named facts, and anything else a reader could check and catch wrong. Opinions, transitions, and your own framing don't need a source; the checkable facts do. A material claim you can't trace to an opened source is a claim you're taking on faith, and Claude's faith isn't evidence. The fix is to reverse the usual order: gather your sources *first*, draft *from* them, and for each material claim open the actual page and confirm it. You keep a running list: claim, source link, date opened. That list is what turns "I think this is right" into "here's where I checked."

Opening a page isn't the whole job. Four more checks decide whether a source actually holds the claim up:

- **Source authority.** Is it trustworthy and ideally first-hand (the city archive, not a forum guess)? A confident-looking page can still be wrong.
- **Corroboration.** For anything load-bearing, find a second independent source that agrees. One page can be mistaken; two independent ones rarely share the same mistake.
- **Quotation accuracy.** If you quote, the words match the original exactly, in context, not paraphrased into something the speaker didn't say.
- **Privacy, copyright, and organizational policy.** Don't publish private or personal data, don't copy protected text as your own, and follow your organization's rules about what may go out under its name.

**Gate the publish.** Even sourced drafts need a human pass before they go live, because sourcing catches wrong facts but not every problem: tone, framing, a claim that's technically true but misleading. Publishing is a one-way door: once it's out, the correction never fully catches up to the original. So a person reads the draft before it ships, every time. Two more things ride along with the gate: **disclose** where AI helped if your audience expects to be told, and **write for readers, not for volume**: don't let Claude's speed tempt you into mass-producing thin pages chasing search traffic, which platforms penalize and readers distrust.

## The rule

<Callout variant="framework" label="Source Every Material Claim, Gate the Publish">

1. **Collect sources first.**
2. **Draft from them,** marking which material claim uses which source.
3. **Open the sources** for every material factual claim: dates, statistics, named facts.
4. **Vet each source:** authoritative and first-hand; corroborated by a second independent source for load-bearing facts; quotations exact.
5. **Clear it to publish:** no private data, no copied protected text, within your organization's rules.
6. **Human review** before publishing.
7. **Disclose** AI help where your audience expects it.
8. **Write for readers first.** SEO is one goal among many; never mass-produce thin pages.

</Callout>

## Try it

You're the gate on a post about your city's new park. Four claims came back from
Claude, each with something attached. For **each**, decide: does it clear, yes or
no, and if no, name the exact fix. Don't just look for "has a source": judge the
**authority** of the source, whether a load-bearing claim is **corroborated**, and
whether a **quotation** matches the original. **Write your four calls in the box
below before you reveal.**

1. "The park cost $4.2M." Source: a comment on a neighborhood forum.
2. "The park opened in 1935." One source: the city archive page, opened and
   checked. This date anchors the whole post.
3. The mayor "called it the city's proudest project in a decade." You open the
   press release; the actual line is "a project we're proud of."
4. "The park spans 12 acres." Two sources opened and agreeing: the city parks
   page and the county land register.

<ExerciseReveal prompt="Reveal the answers" commit="Write your yes/no and the fix for all four, then reveal:">
<Fragment slot="answer">

1. No. **Authority fails:** a forum comment isn't trustworthy or first-hand. Fix: replace it with an authoritative first-hand source (city budget or parks page), or cut the claim.
2. No, not yet. The source is authoritative, but this is the load-bearing fact and it rests on **one source**. Fix: find a second independent source that agrees (a news report, the county register) before it ships.
3. No. **Quotation accuracy fails:** "the city's proudest project in a decade" is not what the release says. Fix: quote the exact words ("a project we're proud of") or drop the quote; don't publish the paraphrase as a quote.
4. Yes. Authoritative, corroborated by a second independent source, no quote to misstate. It clears the sourcing move (a human still gates the publish).

</Fragment>
</ExerciseReveal>

Now run the vetting on your own work. Take one real material claim you'd publish
and carry it through all three checks: (1) name its source and judge its
**authority** (trustworthy, ideally first-hand?); (2) name the **second
independent source** that corroborates it, or state it isn't load-bearing; (3) if
it carries a quote, confirm the words **match the original exactly**, or note
there's no quote. **Write the claim and your three results in the box, then
reveal.**
