<Callout variant="objective" label="Learning objective">

By the end of this lesson you can introduce AI to a team with a small pilot, weekly feedback, data rules, and a pre-set date to decide whether to expand, adjust, or stop.

</Callout>

## Why this matters

Everything so far has been about *you* using Claude well. Rolling it out to a team is a different problem, and the instinct that feels generous ("give everyone access, let them figure it out") is the one that reliably fails. A big-bang launch dumps an unfamiliar tool on dozens of people with no shared use case, no data rules, and no one to ask. Usage spikes on day one out of curiosity, then collapses by week two because nobody found a repeatable win before the novelty wore off.

The alternative is boring and it works: start small, learn cheaply, and decide on evidence. A pilot with a handful of volunteers surfaces the real problems (the prompt that misses something, the data someone shouldn't have pasted) while they're still cheap to fix and affect five people, not five hundred. This lesson gives you the shape of that pilot.

## Why the big launch fails and the pilot sticks

Two rollouts, same tool, opposite outcomes.

**Pam** announces a new AI tool to the whole company in a single email: here's the link, go. Usage spikes as people click in to see what it is, then collapses within two weeks. Nothing was *wrong* with the tool, but nobody was given a specific job to do with it, no one knew what data was off-limits, and when the first person hit a bad result there was no feedback loop to fix it. The failure was invisible until the usage graph flatlined, and by then the company's first impression was "we tried AI, it didn't stick."

**Raj** rolls out the same tool to **five volunteers** for a **two-week pilot** on **one use case**: summarizing meeting notes. Because it's small, he can watch it closely. **Week one** feedback: the summaries miss action items. That's a fixable problem: he adds a rule to the prompt and a checklist, and it's fixed for everyone in the pilot by the next day. **Week two**: two of the five save real time, one finds it slower, two are unsure. He also set **data rules up front**, checking the notes against his company's approved data classification and its AI policy to decide what was allowed into the tool, rather than assuming "no client names means nothing sensitive." (A meeting note with no client name can still carry salary figures, health details, or an unannounced deal, so the absence of a name is not a safe proxy for the absence of sensitive data.) And he set his **thresholds before starting**: *success* was "at least half the pilot saves measurable time with no data-rule violation," *stop* was "any sensitive-data leak, or nobody saves time," *expand* was "success met and the fixes held for a full week." At the end he has measurements against a bar he set in advance, not impressions, and the numbers clear his expand bar, so he **expands to ten people, not fifty**.

The pilot didn't just avoid Pam's crash. It *produced* things the big launch never could: a fixed prompt, real usage numbers, and a worked example other people can copy.

<Callout variant="mistake" label="Common mistake">

Launching to everyone at once because giving the whole team access feels generous. With no shared use case, no data rules, and nobody to ask, usage spikes on curiosity and collapses by week two, and the company's first impression becomes "we tried AI, it didn't stick." Start with a handful of volunteers on one task, where problems stay cheap, visible, and fixable for five people instead of five hundred.

</Callout>

## The six moves of a rollout that sticks

Raj's pilot is a sequence, and the order matters more than it looks.

- **Pick one use case** with a clear benefit. One task everyone understands beats a vague "use it for whatever": it gives you something specific to measure and improve.
- **Run a small pilot** with volunteers. Small keeps problems cheap and visible; volunteers mean you're learning from people who want it to work, not fighting resistance.
- **Set the rules first.** What data is allowed, who to ask when stuck, *before* anyone starts, because a pilot uses real work and real data from day one. Decide what's allowed by checking against your organization's approved data classification and AI policy, not by a gut rule like "no client names" (a note with no name can still hold salary, health, or deal-sensitive data). Waiting until the tool "proves useful" means weeks of unprotected data.
- **Set the thresholds first, too.** Before the pilot starts, write down what *success*, *stop*, and *expand* each look like as a number or a clear line, so the decision at the end is a comparison, not an argument. A stop threshold (for example, any sensitive-data violation) lets you end a pilot that's going wrong without re-litigating it.
- **Collect feedback weekly.** The weekly loop is what turns a complaint ("summaries miss action items") into a fix while the pilot is still small enough to change.
- **Measure** the time saved and the quality, the way [Elective 2](/lessons/electives/e2-measuring-ais-impact/) teaches, so the decision rests on numbers, not on who's most enthusiastic.
- **Decide by a date set in advance:** expand, adjust, or stop, against the thresholds you set. Setting the date and the bar up front is what stops a pilot from drifting forward on momentum instead of evidence.

## The rule

<Callout variant="framework" label="Pilot, Feedback, Expand">

1. **Pick one use case** with a clear benefit.
2. **Run a small pilot** with volunteers.
3. **Set rules:** what data is allowed (by your approved data classification and AI policy, not a gut rule), who to ask.
4. **Set thresholds:** what success, stop, and expand each mean, written down before you start.
5. **Collect feedback** weekly.
6. **Measure** as in [Elective 2](/lessons/electives/e2-measuring-ais-impact/).
7. **Decide:** expand, adjust, or stop, against your thresholds, by a date you set in advance.

</Callout>
